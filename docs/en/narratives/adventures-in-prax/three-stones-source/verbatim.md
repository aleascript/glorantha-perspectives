### The Three Stones Spring

The sun is sinking over the Wastes when you reach the Three Stones Spring. It is a water hole ringed with reeds, guarded by three standing blocks that the elders of your tribe say are older than Waha himself. Fta-Ah pulls at the reins, thirsty.

But she does not drink.

She lowers her muzzle to the water, snorts, then steps back. The other antelopes do the same. Around the pool, the mud bears hobnailed bootprints, and someone has driven stakes connected by a red cord in a straight line to the foot of the tallest of the three stones.

Farther away stands a Lunar camp. Two square tents, a cart loaded with picks and baskets, about ten porters. A man in an ochre tunic, wax tablet in hand, measures the stone's shadow with a graduated pole. Beside him, armed, stand six Sable Riders. You recognize the first: **Hirrak, your elder brother**, sent by your father.

Hirrak sees you arrive and comes to meet you, pleased to see you.

— Little sister! Good timing. The Khan promised the Empire we would escort them here. They are going to dig a cistern for their road. There will be water all year round, and money for the tribe. Father says this is how the protectors of the Moon are rewarded.

He lowers his voice.

— They start at dawn. The big stone has to be moved.

Behind him, the spring water remains still. No insect dances over it. You feel the presence of the spring spirit, the one your tribe calls **Mother-of-Reeds**. She is usually peaceful. Tonight she is silent like someone holding back anger.

And above the Wastes, the **Red Moon rises, full**. Tonight you must sacrifice to your ancestors.

**What do you do?**

— The garrison wants water, not stones. The cistern will be dug here, thirty paces downstream, where the ground remains good. The three stones will not be touched, nor any root of these reeds. And if my porters go near them, your brother has my permission to drive them away.

He looks at you.

— But the cistern will be built at this spring, daughter of the Khan. Not at Jackal Spring. My convoys will not make a detour for my scruples, nor for yours.

You place a hand in the water. Mother-of-Reeds shudders when the porters pass, then calms as they move downstream without touching the stones. She stays.

Hirrak lets out a long breath as though he had been holding it since yesterday.

Then Fta-Ah raises her head and looks east.

On the horizon of the Wastes, in the rising light, a cloud of dust approaches. Several antelopes at a gallop. At their head is a figure carrying a staff adorned with feathers and skulls, one you would know anywhere: **your tribe's shaman**. And beside him, another broad figure holding the reins high as he always does.

**Paak the Cunning.**

The ancestors were heard. But your father is arriving at a camp where, this time, there is nothing left to prevent.

Hirrak has gone pale.

— Little sister. What exactly did your ancestors tell him?

**[PEEK]**

My face lights up. My heart is racing. I have rarely felt so aligned with my convictions: the bond of the Ancestors and our role toward the Goddess. I join the group of nomads. I see familiar faces. I savor my victory, our victory, everyone's. I quickly explain the whole situation to Paak and the shaman and why they received the Ancestors' call. I thank them for coming so quickly but explain everything: the miracle of the white moon, the compromise obtained, and above all I do not blame my brother; on the contrary, I say he helped me greatly. And I finish by looking at Ostar, who has come closer: "We are One."

**[TIME]**

Paak jumps from his mount before it has fully stopped. He is covered in dust, and his eyes first seek your face, then Hirrak's, checking that his children are whole.

You speak quickly. The ancestors, Mother-of-Reeds, the cord, the white moon, Ostar's agreement. And Hirrak, who spoke first before the surveyor. Your brother lowers his eyes when you say it, but you see his shoulders straighten.

The shaman stopped listening as soon as you said "white moon." He has gone to the spring and is looking at the water, where only a red reflection remains in the rising day.

— My grandmother saw it once, he says without turning. The night before the Sables first met the people of the Moon. She never knew what it foretold.

He says no more. He rests his staff against the tallest of the three stones and begins to sing quietly to Mother-of-Reeds.

Ostar has approached. He greets Paak as one greets an ally of the Empire.

— Honor to the Khan. Your daughter told me yesterday that you sent her to inspect the works. She did her job well.

Hirrak freezes. You feel your brother's eyes on you.

Paak does not hesitate for an instant. He lays a heavy hand on your shoulder.

— Of course I sent her. My children see for me where I am not.

Then he turns to the surveyor, and in the same sentence he is already negotiating: the Sables will guard the cistern in exchange for first watering rights and a share of what the convoys pay. Ostar smiles; he has recognized a bargainer.

When you say, "We are One," Ostar stops. He looks at you, surprised, then joins his hands.

— We are One, daughter of the Khan.

Later, when Paak's men are setting camp near the spring, your father takes you aside. His hand is still on your shoulder, but his voice has changed.

— I sent you nowhere, Peek.

He lets a moment pass.

— Today you spoke with my voice, and I said it was mine. Next time, I decide when you speak in my name.

Then, almost despite himself, he smiles.

— You saw farther than your brother. And farther than I did, tonight.

**What do you answer?**

**[PEEK]**

"I learned from a good teacher, Father," I say, looking at him the way a daughter looks at a father she deeply admires.

**[TIME]**

Paak bursts out laughing, the frank laugh that makes the whole camp turn around.

— A good teacher... Here is a daughter who lies as well as her father. And who knows when truth must be spoken.

He hugs you for one second, then releases you at once, because a Khan does not linger in front of his men.
