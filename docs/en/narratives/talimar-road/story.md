# Story — becoming a Grey Sage

Tal had grown up among the Orlanthi of the Grey Heron clan, in the Colymar tribe. He knew the stories, obligations, gods, and old angers of his people. This world was his. Yet something no longer quite held together.

It was not the certainty of a rebel. Tal had traveled too much for that.

His uncle **Grimar**, a merchant, had taken him along the roads of Tarsh from a young age. There he discovered streets that resisted the rain, baths, statues, fine cloth, great buildings, and a civilization sometimes capable of considering several sides of the same conflict. He liked those things. That was precisely the problem.

Because beneath the same Moon there were also slaves, domination, and the Crimson Bat.

His people were right to hate some things about the Empire. Yet the Lunars were not only the monsters of clan stories. Tal no longer knew how to fit all these truths into a single account.

One evening on the road back from Tarsh, still young and excited, he had marveled to Grimar about the buildings, statues, and clothes — especially the women's clothes, he added while blushing. His uncle laughed, then grew more serious.

> “When you admire a great house, always ask yourself who carried the stones.”

Tal never forgot it.

## A road that was not Issaries' road

Everyone could have assumed he would become a merchant. He already knew the roads, some Tarshite customs, the gestures of hospitality, and the price of caution far from home. Grimar loved him. His family had the means to help him take that path.

But goods bored him.

On the road, Tal asked why a stele stood where it did, who had lived there before, why Tarshites told a familiar story differently, how two peoples could honor the same god while keeping different myths.

Behind all those questions another kept returning: **how can we know what is true?**

Lhankor Mhy began to look like a door. Not yet a vocation. A possibility.

## The stone beside the road

One day, while Tal was returning from Tarsh with Grimar, the wind was rising and the road was still long. Beside the road, an old stone had recently been uncovered from the earth. An old man stood before it copying its marks onto a tablet.

Tal wanted to stop.

Grimar would rather have continued, but his nephew's curiosity, their years of travel, and the offer to share salt under Heortling hospitality won out.

Tal approached the old man.

The man handed him his tablet.

> **“Tell me what is wrong.”**

Tal examined the stone, then the copy. He found seven differences.

The bounded problem calmed him almost immediately. For a moment the world became something one could inspect precisely.

“The tablet is wrong,” he concluded. “The stone is older.”

The old man looked up.

“Why not the stone?”

Tal froze.

The stone was older than the tablet. It was not necessarily older than whoever had carved it. Perhaps the inscription itself copied something else. Perhaps it had been corrected, transformed, or made false. Tal had jumped from an observed difference to a story he treated as certain.

What he truly knew was smaller: **the stone and the tablet did not say exactly the same thing.**

The old man introduced himself: **Hendrik, son of Enderos, Grey Sage of Lhankor Mhy.**

Tal answered with a genealogy far too long: Tal, Talimar, son of Calimar and Urestona, carls of the Grey Heron clan, of the Colymar tribe.

Hendrik did not seem troubled by it.

## Wherever a question forces you to go

Tal immediately questioned the Sage about his travels. Hendrik had read Lunar texts, spoken to their priests and scholars, traveled through Tarsh, Esrolia, Prax, part of the Holy Country, and met Westerners.

At last he noticed that Tal kept asking how far he had gone rather than what he had learned there.

“Because that is where I want to go too,” Tal said.

Hendrik shook his head.

> “If you only want to go far, stay with your uncle. Merchants travel more roads than many sages.”

Then he added:

> **“I go wherever a question forces me to go.”**

That night Tal spoke at length with Grimar.

At last he told him that he did not want trade. What he loved on the road was not what could be bought or sold, but what he did not yet understand.

An agreement became possible. His parents could provide food, clothes, and writing materials; Hendrik would receive a contribution; Tal would keep obligations to his family and clan.

Hendrik, for his part, did not promise to make him a Grey Sage.

He promised to teach him to read, write, search, distinguish what he knew from what he thought he knew, and preserve what others had learned. Only then would they discover whether he could become more.

A few weeks later Tal left the Grey Heron. His mother prepared his belongings. His father gave him an ancestral knife. Grimar gave him a purse and far too much advice.

Hendrik gave him the tablet of the seven differences.

Then he asked his first question as a teacher:

> **“Where are we going?”**

Tal immediately proposed a temple, a storehouse of tablets, the Underworld, the edge of the world, the other Lightbringers, the fall of the world's waters, perhaps even victory over the Red Moon.

Hendrik walked on without answering.

## Three accounts and two possible truths

Seasons later, Tal was still an apprentice when Hendrik laid before him three testimonies of the same conflict between clans.

They contradicted one another.

Tal read the first and quickly saw its bias. The second corrected some things and perhaps distorted others. The third opened yet another reading.

He managed to reconstruct a sequence that explained all three texts.

Then he stopped.

Another reconstruction was possible. It too explained all three testimonies.

The other apprentices waited for him to choose.

Tal refused.

He could show that both hypotheses held. He could not claim to know which was true.

Hendrik looked at him differently from that day on.

Tal had learned something difficult: **“I do not know” is not the end of inquiry. Sometimes it is inquiry's first honest sentence.**

## Why we write

Another day, Hendrik gathered his apprentices and told them to set their tablets aside.

“You will write it later. First you need to know why we write.”

Then he told them of Lhankor Mhy.

The god was born from **Orenoar, Mistress of Truth**, upon **Mostal, the Mountain of Law**. Some traditions named Acos as his father; others the Mountain itself. Hendrik did not choose for his students.

He told how Lhankor Mhy knew cosmic Law, how the world entered Darkness, how the Light of Knowledge vanished with Elasa, and how the god invented writing so that what had been learned could survive the absence of the one who taught it.

He told of the Lightbringers.

And he insisted on something Tal did not expect: **Lhankor Mhy sometimes succeeded. And sometimes he failed.**

The god of knowledge was not a shortcut to a perfect answer.

Hendrik turned to Tal.

“You once said, ‘Only Lhankor Mhy knows.’ Now that you know this story, why do you think he lets us search?”

Tal thought.

> **“Because he himself, who knows everything, keeps searching. It is his quest; it is our quest.”**

Hendrik remained silent.

“Yes.”

It was one of the rare times he corrected nothing.

When Tal took up his tablet again, he did not merely record the events of the myth. He wrote: distinguish what is true; remember what has been learned; preserve knowledge through writing; seek what is missing; continue searching even when one already knows.

Hendrik read over his shoulder.

“Keep them. But do not make the mistake of thinking that because you have written what Lhankor Mhy did, you already know what it means to do it.”

## Air, Harmony, Movement

Several more seasons passed.

One morning Hendrik sent the apprentices outside.

“Seek nothing.”

“And what are we supposed to bring back?” someone asked.

“What you find.”

Tal climbed as high as he could.

His mind had been boiling for months. Up there the wind reminded him of his clan. Far from the Grey Heron, his roots felt almost more tangible than before.

He looked at the world in layers: earth below him, Air where he stood, sky, Yelm, stars, Moon. Then he thought of what lay below, where Lhankor Mhy himself had walked.

He felt himself in the middle.

Small, but not crushed.

When he returned, Tal thought he could name three things moving through him.

**Air**, first: the middle, the interface, what exists between high and low.

**Harmony**: what could bind different, even contrary things without making them disappear.

**Movement**: the curiosity that would not let him stand still before a question.

Hendrik asked what Harmony meant if opposites truly remained opposites.

Tal answered with the words that would remain his:

> **“Things are. Even when contrary, they are there, together.”**

The three Runes were written onto his tablet: **Air. Harmony. Movement.**

## What remains without the tablet

A few seasons later Hendrik presented Tal for initiation into Lhankor Mhy.

At the sanctuary entrance, the Sages took away everything he had written.

Even the tablet bearing his three Runes.

“You do not take what you know with you. You are about to discover what remains when you cannot consult it.”

Tal entered.

Before him were the signs of **Truth** and **Law**.

A Sage asked:

> **“What do you seek here?”**

Tal said he wanted the agreement and support of the first of sages, the grey-bearded god, keeper of knowledge. If accepted, he would devote his life to Law and Truth.

Then his own doubt struck him.

Neither Law nor Truth was on his tablet.

Had he spent all this time becoming someone else? Was he in the wrong place?

The Sages did not answer for him.

They asked whether he came to offer his life to Lhankor Mhy or to the Runes. They reminded him that a man did not need to be the Wind to worship Orlanth. And a man who already possessed Truth might have no reason to come seeking it.

Then Tal understood.

What he had called **Harmony** was not false. It was the name he had given his intuition of **Law**: what binds things without abolishing their differences.

What he had called **Movement** was not false either. He had finally recognized the direction of that movement: **Truth**, not as certainty acquired, but as what had always compelled him to continue searching.

Tal fell to his knees and wept.

For a moment he no longer knew whether he stood in the sanctuary or elsewhere. He seemed to see a grey-bearded presence amid everything that had been known, forgotten, and remained to be sought.

And the first question returned:

> **“Tell me what is wrong.”**

There was no tablet now.

There was the world.

When Tal returned to himself, the Sages traced **Truth**, then **Law**, on his brow.

Hendrik stood behind them.

He said only:

> **“Now, seek.”**

Talimar's first session ended where his true road began.
